## The one thing to understand first

**Sezzle does not look like a lender on its balance sheet, and that is the whole risk.** Total assets are $456.5M against roughly $4B to $5B of annualised gross merchandise volume. The loans are six-week, four-instalment, self-liquidating consumer credit, funded through a revolving facility (a new $300M line was secured at Q2). The book turns over roughly eight times a year, so a small balance sheet can support enormous volume.

The consequence: **there is $233M of equity underneath billions of dollars of consumer credit flowing through the business each year.** When losses are normal this is spectacular capital efficiency and it is why ROIC is 49.6%. When losses spike, the hit arrives in the income statement almost immediately, because the assets reprice in weeks rather than years. There is no long-duration book to run off and no reserve cushion of consequence.

## How it actually makes money, and how that changed

Classic BNPL earns a merchant discount fee plus consumer late fees. Sezzle has been moving away from that: **active subscribers rose 76.4% year over year to 854,000**, and subscription products (Sezzle Premium, Anywhere) are an increasing share of revenue, with SezzleCash launched and Sezzle Send planned.

That matters more than the growth rate. A subscription dollar is recurring and is not underwritten against a consumer's ability to repay a specific loan. To the degree the mix keeps shifting, the business becomes less of a lender and more of a consumer fintech subscription. **That shift is the actual bull case**, not the 51.7% headline growth.

## Valuation, stated plainly

At the 2026-08-27 close of 125.80: forward P/E 23.9x on FY26E EPS of $5.26 and 18.9x on FY27E of $6.65, forward PEG 0.91. Street target $168.

**Price to book is roughly 18x.** You are paying $4.2B for $233M of equity. That is a software multiple on a consumer lender. It is defensible only if the subscription mix shift is real and durable, and it means **there is no asset floor under the price**, the same structural point flagged on WMG on 2026-08-26.

**Short interest is 23.0% of float.** A quarter of the tradeable shares are positioned against this. **Insiders own 52.7%**, so the float is small and founder control is absolute. Both facts cut both ways and both belong in the position sizing, which is now fixed.

## Bear case, in one paragraph

Subprime consumer credit is a late-cycle business and this is late in a cycle. The single line that determines the outcome, transaction losses as a percentage of GMV, is not in the database and was not checked before the buy. Q2 net income fell 20% sequentially on rising revenue. Management guided second-half growth down, which is what the 33.9% decline was actually about. The equity cushion is $233M against billions of annual originations, funded on a revolver that has to be renewed and repriced. You are paying 18x book with no asset floor, and 23% of the float is short.

## Bull case, in one paragraph

Revenue growing 51.7% with a 61.1% FCF margin and 49.6% ROIC, funded without issuing a share in two years, while the revenue mix shifts from underwritten transaction fees toward recurring subscription from 854,000 subscribers growing 76.4%. Full-year guidance was raised, not cut, to the high end of 35% growth with adjusted net income raised to $185M. Debt fell and equity rose in the most recent quarter. Insiders own 52.7% and one bought in the open market with zero discretionary selling across 85 measured rows in 180 days. At 18.9x next year's earnings and a PEG under 1, the price already embeds the deceleration the market sold it for.

## What this brief cannot tell you, named rather than glossed

1. **Transaction losses as a percentage of GMV, and the trend.** This is the number that decides the thesis and it lives in the 10-Q, not the database. Without it the trigger prongs written into [[Holdings]] are drafted from a press release rather than from the underwriting book.
2. **Whether the loan book is held, sold, or financed**, and on what terms the $300M facility sits (advance rate, covenants, repricing schedule). Receivables read $0 in the last three quarters against $3M to $7M before, which is a presentation change this brief cannot resolve.
3. **What caused the sequential net income decline.** Investment spend, credit, or funding cost are three very different answers with three very different consequences.
4. **Regulatory exposure.** BNPL sits under active CFPB and state attention on whether instalment products are credit under the Truth in Lending Act. Not researched here.

Any one of these could reverse the verdict. **This is an essential-information brief, not the full deep dive the regime requires.**
