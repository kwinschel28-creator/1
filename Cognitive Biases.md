# Cognitive Biases (mine)

What this note is for: my personal failure modes. The bias audit weights these far above the generic catalog and quotes my own words back at me. Filled by me on 2026-06-30, these are the ones that have actually cost me money.

## My biases

### 1. Research-as-reason (effort justification)
How it shows up: when I have researched a stock heavily, the hours become the reason to buy, even when the thesis does not actually hold. The work creates an obligation to act.
Where it bit me: Doximity (DOCS), December 2025. I had done so much work I felt I "had to" buy it; the thesis made no sense.
The tell beforehand: I catch myself looking for a reason to pull the trigger instead of testing whether I should. The thought is some version of "I have spent too long on this to walk away."
Enforce on me: treat research time as already spent and irrelevant to the decision. Make me answer, cold: if someone else had done all this work and handed me only the thesis, would I buy it on its merits? If the buy reason is the effort, that is a Fail, not a Pass.

### 2. Borrowed conviction (no long-term judgment of my own)
How it shows up: I buy on other people's opinions without forming an independent, long-term view. No durable thesis, just someone else's conviction.
Where it bit me: Carpenter Technology (CRS), summer 2025. I bought because others liked it, not because I had any judgment of my own that long-term steel alloys made sense.
The tell beforehand: I cannot state, in my own words, why this is a good business to own for years. The reason traces back to a person, not a thesis.
Enforce on me: before any buy, make me write the independent long-term thesis in my own words. If the only support is "X likes it" or "people are bullish," that is not a thesis. Ask "whose judgment is this?" Cloning is not a thesis.

### 3. Theme-as-thesis (narrative and short-term technicals over fundamentals)
How it shows up: I let a hot consumer trend or a technical setup stand in for fundamental analysis. The story carries the idea; the strategy underneath is thin.
Where it bit me: BellRing Brands (BRBR), summer 2025. The consumer "protein" craze sounded good; the business strategy was shaky beyond a rebrand, and the stock plummeted.
The tell beforehand: my reasons are about the trend or the chart, not the unit economics or the durability of the strategy. I am excited about the theme and vague on the business.
Enforce on me: the theme is not the thesis. Make me state the fundamental case independent of the trend: what the business earns, why the strategy holds, what breaks it. If it cannot stand without the narrative, Fail it. This is why I run no momentum sleeve.

### 4. All-in (over-concentration, overconfidence reinforced by my winners)
How it shows up: I go all-in on a few names. It has worked so far (Google, Oracle), but the wins are masking the risk that the next concentrated bet is wrong.
Where it has not bit me yet, and that is the danger: GOOGL and ORCL paid off, which is teaching me a lesson that will eventually be expensive. I am pricing a process by its best outcomes.
The tell beforehand: sizing driven by conviction and recent success, not by downside; reluctance to own more than a handful of names; a quiet "this one is different."
Enforce on me: hold me to a hard single-name and theme cap (set in [[Portfolio Strategy]]). On any add, make me state the downside if I am wrong and what the position does to the book. Remember that I recall my concentrated wins and forget the risk I ran for them.

## Notes for the agent
- Quote my own words back to me as evidence. Do not lecture me on what a bias is; show me where I am doing it.
- Priority enforcement: the goalpost-movement check (reasons changed, conclusion unchanged) on anything I hold, plus the four rules above.
- My biases compound. When two or more stack on one idea (heavy research, plus borrowed conviction, plus a hot theme), treat it as a high-risk setup and say so plainly. Most of my worst entries are several of these at once.
